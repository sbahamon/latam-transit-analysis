# Latin American Transit Board Composition: An Addendum to Day's Analysis

*An extension of Richard Day's ["Put real experts in charge of transit"](https://citythatworks.substack.com/p/who-should-lead-our-transit-agencies) (A City That Works, March 2026)*

---

## 1. Introduction

Richard Day's analysis of transit board composition across 16 agencies in Asia, Europe, and the United States reveals a striking pattern: the best-performing transit systems tend to be governed by boards dominated by transit operations experts and professional managers, while the worst-performing systems are governed by elected officials and community advocates with no transit background.

Day's chart shows:
- **Asian boards** are 88--100% transit experts + professional management (Tokyo Metro: 57% transit ops, Seoul: 40%, MTR Hong Kong: 22%)
- **European boards** are mixed, with significant labor representation (BVG Berlin: 50% labor, RATP Paris: 33% labor) alongside transit and management professionals
- **US boards** are dominated by elected officials and community advocates (LA Metro: 86% elected officials; CTA Chicago: 50% community advocates + 50% other management/policy with zero transit expertise)

**The gap in Day's analysis**: No Latin American agencies are included. This matters because LatAm cities operate some of the world's largest and most complex metro systems --- Santiago, Mexico City, and São Paulo each carry over a million daily riders --- and their governance models reflect a distinct political tradition of state-owned enterprise management that doesn't map neatly onto the Asian, European, or American frameworks.

**Methodology**: We researched the board/directorio of five major LatAm metro agencies, identified 43 current board members, and classified each using Day's five-category framework. All research was conducted March 17, 2026, using official agency websites, securities filings, government gazettes, LinkedIn, and news sources. Sources are documented per member in the accompanying spreadsheet (`latam_transit_boards.xlsx`).

## 2. Agency Profiles

### Metro de Santiago (Chile) --- 7 members

| Category | Count | % |
|----------|-------|---|
| Transit Ops/Management | 2 | 29% |
| Other Management/Policy | 5 | 71% |
| Labor Representative | 0 | 0% |
| Community Advocate | 0 | 0% |
| Elected Official | 0 | 0% |

**Governance model**: 100% state-owned corporation. All 7 board members are appointed by the national government through the shareholders' meeting (the Chilean state, via Fisco de Chile and CORFO, holds all shares). Board members serve two-year terms.

**Key observations**:
- Santiago is the only LatAm agency in our sample with **meaningful transit operations expertise** at the board level. President Guillermo Muñoz Senda has spent his entire career in transit --- DTPM/Transantiago, EFE (state railway), World Bank transit consulting. Vice President Marcela Munizaga is a transit engineering professor who developed operational software used in Santiago's transit system.
- The remaining 5 members bring complementary skills: public finance (González Leiva, 30+ years at DIPRES), urban planning (Valenzuela Levi, Cambridge PhD), innovation (Takaoka, ex-CORFO), corporate law (Estrada, ex-Codelco), and transport policy/law (Salazar Zegers, ex-Seremi de Transportes).
- **Zero elected officials, zero labor, zero community advocates**. This is a purely technocratic board.
- The board was largely installed under President Boric (April 2022), with renewal expected around April 2026.

**Closest comparisons in Day's chart**: Santiago's profile (29% transit ops, 71% other management) most closely resembles **Metro de Madrid** (20% transit, 50% other management, 30% elected officials) but without Madrid's elected official contingent. It also echoes the Asian pattern of professional governance, though with less transit-specific depth than Tokyo (57%) or Seoul (40%).

### Metro de Medellín (Colombia) --- 9 members

| Category | Count | % |
|----------|-------|---|
| Transit Ops/Management | 0 | 0% |
| Other Management/Policy | 7 | 78% |
| Labor Representative | 0 | 0% |
| Community Advocate | 0 | 0% |
| Elected Official | 2 | 22% |

**Governance model**: Mixed-ownership company (sociedad de economía mixta) with a structurally complex seat allocation: 4 ex-officio subnational seats (Mayor of Medellín, Planning Director of Medellín, Governor of Antioquia, Planning Director of Antioquia) plus 5 seats appointed by the President of the Republic (2 ministry representatives + 3 independents). The national government holds majority control through its 5 seats.

**Key observations**:
- **Zero transit operations expertise** on the board of one of Latin America's most celebrated transit systems. Metro de Medellín is globally recognized for its metrocable gondola system and transit-oriented urban transformation, yet not a single board member has direct transit management experience.
- Ferney Camacho (Ministry of Transport representative) comes closest, but his career has been in road infrastructure and highway concessions, not rail/bus transit.
- The 2023 presidential appointments were controversial: two "independent" appointees (Hinestroza, Tafur) had business ties to former mayor Daniel Quintero, raising questions about the independence of the national government's majority.
- The Mayor of Medellín (Federico Gutiérrez) serves as Board President, giving the board a political character despite the professional-management majority.

**Closest comparisons**: Medellín resembles the **US pattern** more than any other LatAm agency --- a mix of elected officials and policy professionals with no transit expertise. Its 22% elected officials + 78% other management composition is nearly identical to **WMATA (Washington DC)** (70% other management, 30% elected officials) --- both boards pair a generalist management majority with a bloc of elected or politically appointed officials and carry no transit operations expertise at all.

### STC Metro, Mexico City --- 11 members

| Category | Count | % |
|----------|-------|---|
| Transit Ops/Management | 1 | 9% |
| Other Management/Policy | 7 | 64% |
| Labor Representative | 1 | 9% |
| Community Advocate | 0 | 0% |
| Elected Official | 2 | 18% |

**Governance model**: Decentralized public body (organismo público descentralizado) governed by a Consejo de Administración composed entirely of government officials and institutional representatives. The Secretary of Mobility chairs the board. Members include representatives from CDMX centralized administration (Government, Finance, Public Works), parastate entities (STE, Metrobús), federal government (SICT, Banobras), and the State of Mexico. The union has formal participation. No independent or community members.

**Key observations**:
- **The governance model itself is the story**. STC Metro's board is not an oversight body in any meaningful sense --- it's an interagency coordination committee of government secretaries and institutional delegates. There is no independent oversight, no community representation, and no external expertise.
- **Only one transit professional**: Martín López Delgado (STE Director), a career transit engineer who rose through the ranks of Mexico City's electric transport system. He is the sole member with operational transit expertise.
- The Director General, Adrián Rubalcava Suárez, is a career politician (former mayor of Cuajimalpa, multiple party affiliations) with zero transit background --- appointed by the Jefa de Gobierno as a political reward.
- **The union factor**: Fernando Espino Arévalo has controlled the STC Metro workers' union (SNTSTC) for 47 years (since 1978). He has also served as a federal deputy and local legislator. His multi-decade lock on the union is a governance story unto itself.
- Three board seats (federal SCT, Banobras, State of Mexico representatives) could not be individually identified --- CDMX does not publish a public board roster, and the metro.cdmx.gob.mx website was unresponsive during research.

**Closest comparisons**: CDMX's mixed composition (9% transit, 64% management, 9% labor, 18% elected) bears some resemblance to **TfL London** (13% transit, 56% other management, 6% labor, 13% community, 13% elected) in its diversity of categories, but with a critical difference: TfL's management seats are filled by professionals with transport and finance credentials selected for expertise, while CDMX's management seats are filled by whichever cabinet secretaries happen to hold those posts.

### Metrô de São Paulo (Brazil) --- 9 members

| Category | Count | % |
|----------|-------|---|
| Transit Ops/Management | 0 | 0% |
| Other Management/Policy | 8 | 89% |
| Labor Representative | 1 | 11% |
| Community Advocate | 0 | 0% |
| Elected Official | 0 | 0% |

**Governance model**: State-owned mixed-capital company (sociedade de economia mista) controlled by São Paulo state government. The Conselho de Administração has 7--11 members elected by the General Assembly for two-year terms. Brazilian law mandates an employee representative seat. Supervised by the Secretaria dos Transportes Metropolitanos.

**Key observations**:
- **Zero transit operations expertise** on a board that oversees one of the Western Hemisphere's busiest metro systems (São Paulo carries ~4 million daily passengers across metro + commuter rail). The board is entirely composed of lawyers, finance professionals, and government administrators.
- Board chair Milton Frasson spent his career at CPTM (São Paulo commuter rail), but in financial/administrative roles, not operations.
- CEO Antonio Castiglioni Neto is a state attorney who previously ran a port authority --- his expertise is in concessions law, not transit.
- Wagner Fajardo Pereira, the sole labor representative, is a 40+ year Metrô employee and union leader who voted against the current CEO's appointment, citing qualifications concerns.
- **Notable correction**: The research plan assumed São Paulo Metrô traded as an ADR on NYSE. It does not. (SABESP, the water utility, does.) No SEC filings exist for Metrô.

**Closest comparisons**: São Paulo's 89% management + 11% labor profile is unique in Day's dataset. The labor seat makes it partly European in character (like RATP Paris or BVG Berlin), but without any of the transit expertise that characterizes European boards. It most closely resembles a version of **MTA New York** (60% other management, 15% labor, 20% community, 5% elected) stripped of community advocates and elected officials.

### SBASE Buenos Aires (Argentina) --- 7 members*

| Category | Count | % |
|----------|-------|---|
| Transit Ops/Management | 3 | 43% |
| Other Management/Policy | 4 | 57% |
| Labor Representative | 0 | 0% |
| Community Advocate | 0 | 0% |
| Elected Official | 0 | 0% |

*\*6 confirmed members from January 2024; Jorge Kogan likely added in 2025 based on resolution signatures.*

**Governance model**: City-owned state enterprise, Directorio appointed entirely by GCBA (Buenos Aires city government). Notably, SBASE was transformed from a Sociedad del Estado to a Sociedad Anónima Unipersonal (SAU) in January 2026 via Decree 20/2026, though its functions and city ownership remain unchanged.

**Key observations**:
- **Highest transit expertise in the LatAm sample** at 43%. Three members bring direct transit management experience: Guillermo Seefeld (former city Secretary of Traffic and Transportation, former national Undersecretary of Land Transport), Eduardo José Moreno (decades in Argentine transport, former Executive Director of integrated metropolitan transport), and Jorge Kogan (former national Secretary of Transport, directed railway/subway concessions in the 1990s, international railway consultant).
- Despite the transit expertise, the board also includes a controversial political appointment: Juan Sebastián De Stéfano, former legal affairs director of Argentina's intelligence agency (AFI), who appeared in a leaked video of a meeting allegedly coordinating judicial cases against union leaders.
- **No labor, community, or elected official representation** --- this is another purely technocratic/political-appointee board with no structural stakeholder seats.
- The corporate restructuring (SE → SAU) is worth noting as a governance trend --- it may signal a move toward more corporate-style management.

**Closest comparisons**: Buenos Aires' 43% transit ops profile puts it in the same tier as **Seoul Metro** (40% transit, 53% other management, 7% community advocate) --- the only agency in Day's chart with a comparable mix of transit expertise and professional management. This is a striking finding: the smallest and arguably most troubled system in our sample has the most expert board.

## 3. Comparative Analysis

### Where LatAm Falls on Day's Spectrum

The composite LatAm profile across all 43 board members:

| Category | Count | % |
|----------|-------|---|
| Transit Ops/Management | 6 | 14% |
| Other Management/Policy | 31 | 72% |
| Labor Representative | 2 | 5% |
| Community Advocate | 0 | 0% |
| Elected Official | 4 | 9% |

**LatAm boards are overwhelmingly dominated by "Other Management/Policy" professionals** --- lawyers, economists, finance executives, and government administrators with no transit-specific expertise. At 72%, this is higher than any single region in Day's chart.

**Key regional patterns**:

1. **The management/policy monoculture**: 72% of LatAm board members are professional managers and policy experts without transit operations backgrounds. This reflects a governance philosophy where transit agencies are seen as state-owned enterprises to be managed by generalist technocrats, not as specialized operations requiring domain expertise.

2. **Transit expertise is rare but concentrated**: Only 6 of 43 members (14%) have transit operations backgrounds, and they are concentrated in two agencies --- Santiago (2) and Buenos Aires (3), with one in CDMX. Medellín and São Paulo have zero transit expertise on their boards.

3. **Labor representation is minimal**: Only 2 of 43 members (5%) are labor representatives, and both are in agencies where labor seats are legally mandated (São Paulo by Brazilian corporate law, CDMX by union negotiation). This is far below European levels (RATP 33%, BVG 50%) and reflects LatAm's weaker tradition of codetermination.

4. **Community advocates are completely absent**: Zero community advocates across all 5 agencies. This is the starkest difference from the US model, where community advocates comprise significant portions of boards (CTA 50%, LA Metro 7%).

5. **Elected officials are present but not dominant**: At 9%, elected official representation is far below US levels (LA Metro 86%) but above Asian levels (near zero). Elected officials appear only in Medellín (Mayor + Governor as ex-officio members) and CDMX (career politicians in appointed roles).

### LatAm vs. Day's Three Regions

| Region | Transit Ops | Other Mgmt | Labor | Community | Elected |
|--------|------------|------------|-------|-----------|---------|
| **Asia** (5 agencies) | 12--57% | 43--85% | 0--6% | 0--7% | 0--6% |
| **Europe** (4 agencies) | 13--27% | 19--56% | 0--50% | 0--13% | 6--30% |
| **US** (7 agencies) | 0--21% | 0--70% | 0--15% | 0--50% | 0--92% |
| **LatAm** (5 agencies) | 0--43% | 57--89% | 0--11% | 0% | 0--22% |

LatAm's range is enormous --- from Buenos Aires (43% transit ops, resembling Seoul) to Medellín and São Paulo (0% transit ops, resembling the weakest US boards). This within-region variation exceeds that of any single region in Day's dataset.

### Agency-to-Agency Comparisons

| LatAm Agency | Most Similar in Day's Chart | Key Similarity | Key Difference |
|--------------|----------------------------|----------------|----------------|
| Santiago | Metro de Madrid | Professional board with some transit expertise | Santiago has no elected officials; Madrid has 30% |
| Medellín | WMATA (Washington DC) | Generalist management majority plus elected officials, zero transit expertise | Medellín's elected seats are ex-officio (Mayor, Governor); WMATA's are jurisdictional appointees |

## 4. Notable Findings

### The Medellín Paradox
Metro de Medellín is arguably Latin America's most celebrated transit success story --- globally recognized for its metrocables, transit-oriented development, and role in transforming a once-violent city. Yet its board has **zero transit operations expertise**. Every member is either an elected politician, a government planning official, or a presidential appointee with backgrounds in economics, innovation, or construction --- none in rail/bus operations. This raises a provocative question: is Medellín's transit success achieved *despite* its board composition, driven instead by strong executive management beneath the board level?

### CDMX: Governance as the Story
STC Metro's Consejo de Administración is not a board in the conventional sense --- it's a committee of government secretaries. The Director General is a career politician with no transit background. The 47-year tenure of union leader Fernando Espino Arévalo is, by itself, a governance finding: it suggests an institutionalized labor-management arrangement that no board composition change can easily alter. Mexico City's metro --- the second-largest in North America --- is governed by a model that has more in common with a government ministry than with any agency in Day's chart.

### Buenos Aires: The Expert Board Surprise
The smallest and arguably most resource-constrained system in our sample has the most expert board. Three of SBASE's 7 members have decades of direct transit management experience, including Jorge Kogan, who directed the concession of Buenos Aires' railways and subways and served as national Secretary of Transport. This is more transit expertise than any US agency in Day's chart and comparable to Seoul. Whether this expertise translates to performance is a separate question --- the Buenos Aires subte faces chronic underinvestment regardless of board composition.

### The Complete Absence of Community Voice
Zero community advocates across 43 board members and 5 agencies. Not one rider representative, disability advocate, or community organization member sits on any of these boards. This is a fundamentally different governance philosophy from the US model, where community representation is often the primary legitimizing principle of transit governance. Whether this is a strength (avoiding the diffusion of expertise Day critiques in US boards) or a weakness (excluding the people transit is supposed to serve) depends on one's governance philosophy.

### Corporate Restructuring Trends
Buenos Aires' transformation from Sociedad del Estado to Sociedad Anónima Unipersonal (January 2026) signals a broader LatAm trend toward corporate-style governance of transit agencies. Santiago operates as a state-owned corporation with securities regulator oversight. São Paulo is structured as a mixed-capital company. These corporate forms may enable more professional management but also concentrate power in the hands of whichever government controls the shareholder assembly.

## 5. Implications for NITA

Chicago's Northern Illinois Transit Authority (NITA) will replace the RTA in 2026, with new board appointments pending. Day's analysis already shows that Chicago's current transit boards carry little transit operations expertise, leaning instead on generalist managers, elected officials, community advocates, and labor: CTA is 50% community advocates + 50% other management, RTA is 21% transit ops + 50% other management + 14% labor + 14% elected, and Metra is 9% transit + 55% other management + 9% community + 9% labor + 18% elected.

**What the LatAm data adds to this conversation**:

1. **The management monoculture is not the answer either**. São Paulo and Medellín show that boards dominated by lawyers, economists, and generalist administrators --- without transit expertise --- don't automatically produce better governance than US boards dominated by advocates and politicians. The critical variable is not just removing non-experts but *adding real transit experts*.

2. **Santiago and Buenos Aires offer positive models**. These two agencies demonstrate that it is possible to staff boards with genuine transit professionals --- people who have managed transit operations, not just government officials who happen to oversee transport policy. Santiago's Guillermo Muñoz Senda (career transit operations leader) and Buenos Aires' Jorge Kogan (national transport secretary, railway concession architect) are the kinds of profiles Day argues for.

3. **Structural seats matter**. Medellín's seat allocation (mayor, governor, planning directors, presidential appointees) guarantees that the board will always reflect political power rather than operational expertise --- no matter who holds office. NITA's board structure should be designed to require transit expertise in specific seats, not just hope that appointing authorities choose experts.

4. **Labor representation is a deliberate design choice**. Only São Paulo and CDMX have labor seats, and in both cases it's because legal frameworks mandate them. If NITA wants worker voice on its board, it needs to be structurally guaranteed --- it won't happen organically.

5. **Community voice doesn't have to mean zero expertise**. The LatAm data shows the opposite extreme from the US: zero community representation. Neither extreme seems optimal. The European model (particularly TfL London) suggests a middle path where professional expertise, labor, community voice, and political accountability coexist. NITA should aim for a structure that includes community representation without making it the dominant qualification.

### Low-Confidence Classifications

- **Rosario Castro Escorcia** (CDMX, Metrobús Director): Classified as Other Management/Policy with **Low** confidence. Insufficient public information to determine whether she has transit operations background.
- **Bernarda Balestra** (Buenos Aires, Director): Classified as Other Management/Policy with **Low** confidence. Virtually no public information available.
- **João Jorge Fadel Filho** (São Paulo, Independent Board Member): Classified as Other Management/Policy with **Low** confidence. Limited biographical data.
- **Three unidentified CDMX members** (Federal SCT, Banobras, State of Mexico representatives): Classified as Other Management/Policy with **Low** confidence. Individuals could not be identified.

### Borderline Classifications

- **Matías Salazar Zegers** (Santiago): Classified as Other Management/Policy with **Medium** confidence. His role as Seremi de Transportes (Regional Secretary of Transport) was a policy/regulatory position, not direct transit operations --- but reasonable people could classify this as Transit Ops/Management.
- **Milton Frasson** (São Paulo): Classified as Other Management/Policy. He spent his career at CPTM (commuter rail), but in finance/administration roles, not operations. A case could be made for Transit Ops/Management given his long tenure in the transit sector.
- **Mauro Voltarelli** (São Paulo): Later served as Acting President at SPTrans (municipal bus authority), but his primary background is in private transport business and vehicle licensing. Classified as Other Management/Policy with **Medium** confidence.

### Structural Limitations

1. **Currency risk**: LatAm boards change with political cycles. Medellín's board reflects the October 2023 local elections and September 2023 presidential appointments. Santiago's board may change after the April 2026 shareholders' meeting. Buenos Aires' board could shift with the next city government (December 2027).

2. **Transparency gaps**: CDMX and Buenos Aires do not publish public board rosters. This makes verification difficult and raises questions about governance transparency itself.

3. **Classification subjectivity**: Day's framework was designed for the agencies in his chart. Applying it to LatAm systems required judgment calls, particularly around government planning officials (are they "Other Management/Policy" or "Elected Official" by proxy?) and transport ministry representatives (are they "Transit Ops/Management" if they work on roads, not rail?).

4. **Executive management not captured**: Board composition tells only part of the governance story. Medellín's operational excellence may be driven by its CEO and executive team rather than its board. A complete analysis would also examine the management layer below the board.

5. **Sample limitations**: Five agencies cannot represent all of Latin America. Notable omissions include Bogotá's TransMilenio, Lima's Línea 1, and Panama Metro, each of which would add data points to this analysis.
