Latin American Transit Board Composition: An Addendum to Day's Analysis

An extension of Richard Day's "Put real experts in charge of transit" (A City That Works, March 2026)

Board rosters verified 27 August 2026. An earlier version of this analysis, based on March 2026 rosters, is archived in data_2026_03/; see §6 for what changed and why.

1Introduction

Richard Day's analysis of transit board composition across 16 agencies in Asia, Europe, and the United States reveals a striking pattern: the best-performing transit systems tend to be governed by boards dominated by transit operations experts and professional managers, while the worst-performing systems are governed by elected officials and community advocates with no transit background.

Day's data shows:

  • Asian boards are 88–100% transit experts + professional management (Tokyo Metro: 57% transit ops, Seoul: 40%, MTR Hong Kong: 22%)
  • European boards are mixed, with significant labor representation (BVG Berlin: 50% labor, RATP Paris: 33% labor) alongside transit and management professionals
  • US boards are dominated by elected officials and community advocates (LA Metro: 86% elected officials; CTA Chicago: 50% community advocates + 50% other management/policy with zero transit expertise)

The gap in Day's analysis: No Latin American agencies are included. This matters because LatAm cities operate some of the world's largest and most complex metro systems — Santiago, Mexico City, and São Paulo each carry over a million daily riders — and their governance models reflect a distinct political tradition of state-owned enterprise management that doesn't map neatly onto the Asian, European, or American frameworks.

Methodology: We identified every current member of the board of five major LatAm metro agencies — 42 people across 5 boards — and classified each using Day's five-category framework. Rosters were first researched in March 2026 and fully re-researched on 27 August 2026 against agency filings, official gazettes, and regulatory disclosures. Sources and a confidence rating are recorded per member in data/. Seven classifications required a judgment call; all seven are documented in §6.

STC Metro — Mexico City, Mexico — 11 members

Governance model: Decentralized public body (organismo público descentralizado) governed by a Consejo de Administración (Board of Directors) composed of government officials and institutional representatives, chaired by the Secretary of Mobility. Per Article 7 of the Estatuto Orgánico (reconstructed from the July 2018 text, which is the most recent version whose full Article 7 text could be retrieved — see notes on statute currency), the statutory voting seats are: from CDMX centralized administration — Secretary of Mobility (President), Secretary of Government, Secretary of Finance (now Secretaría de Administración y Finanzas, which absorbed the Oficial Mayor seat when that post was eliminated in 2019), Secretary of Works and Services; from CDMX parastatal entities — the Director General of Servicio de Transportes Eléctricos (STE) and the head of 'Sistema de Movilidad 1' (the 2016-2019 legal name of RTP/Red de Transporte de Pasajeros, now reverted to RTP but apparently not relabeled in Article 7's text — the seat is held by RTP's current Director General); from the federal government — a representative of SICT (formerly SCT) and a representative of Banobras; and one representative designated by the State of Mexico. Article 7 (2018 text) also references one public commissioner ('comisario público', voice but no vote) and, per a 2001 agreement, two citizen comptrollers ('contralores ciudadanos') — see notes; these are non-voting oversight roles, not counted among the board members below. The Director General of STC is NOT listed as an Article 7 Council seat; available evidence indicates the Director General is the administrator/executive appointed by the Jefa de Gobierno who implements Council resolutions and reports to the Council (commonly functioning as its non-voting secretary in this type of CDMX decentralized body), rather than a voting member — he is nonetheless included below because he is the agency's chief executive and because prior analysis specifically asked that his classification be documented. The union (SNTSTC) has no Article 7 seat; its participation in Council sessions stems from an administrative agreement announced by the STC Director General in 2013-2015 under instruction from then-Head of Government Miguel Ángel Mancera, granting the union attendance at Council sessions and two seats on the Comité de Incidentes Relevantes — not a statutory board seat. A separate Consejo Consultivo (advisory council) of outside experts, academics, and civil-society representatives exists alongside the Consejo de Administración (referenced in modifications from 2018 and again in 2024) but is a distinct body. There are no independent/community-appointed voting members on the Consejo de Administración itself.

  1. The management/policy monoculture: 71% of LatAm board members are professional managers and policy experts without transit operations backgrounds. This reflects a governance philosophy where transit agencies are seen as state-owned enterprises to be managed by generalist technocrats, not as specialized operations requiring domain expertise.
  1. Transit expertise is thin and unevenly spread: 6 of 42 members (14%) have transit operations backgrounds. Four of the five boards have at least one; Buenos Aires has none. Three of the six rest on judgment calls documented in §6, so this figure should be read as an upper bound.
  1. Labor representation is minimal: 2 of 42 members (5%), and both sit in agencies where a labor seat is legally mandated or negotiated (São Paulo by Brazilian corporate law, CDMX by union agreement). This is far below European levels (RATP 33%, BVG 50%).
  1. Community advocates are completely absent: Zero community advocates across all five agencies, in both the March and August rosters. This is the starkest difference from the US model, where community advocates comprise significant portions of boards (CTA 50%, MTA New York 20%).
  1. Elected officials are present but concentrated: At 10%, elected official representation is far below US levels (LA Metro 86%, Pace 92%) but above Asian levels. They appear in only two agencies — Medellín, where the Mayor and Governor sit ex officio, and Mexico City, where two career politicians hold appointed seats.

Yet the aggregate profile barely moved. The March snapshot recorded 14% transit ops / 72% other management / 5% labor / 0% community / 9% elected. The August rosters, with substantially different people in the seats, produce 14% / 71% / 5% / 0% / 10%. Board composition in these agencies appears to be a property of the institutions rather than of the individuals who happen to occupy them — which is the strongest argument in this analysis that governance structure, not appointment luck, determines who ends up on a transit board.

CDMX: Governance as the Story

STC Metro's Consejo de Administración is not a board in the conventional sense — it is a committee of sitting government officials. Of its 11 seats, three are unnamed institutional placeholders (federal transport ministry, Banobras, State of Mexico) whose individual occupants are not publicly identified. The union seat, held by Fernando Espino Arévalo, is not established by the agency's statute at all; it derives from a negotiated agreement and may be session participation rather than a voting seat. Espino has led the union since the 1980s and simultaneously served six terms as a federal or local deputy between 1991 and 2015.

The Complete Absence of Community Voice

Across 42 board members, five agencies, five countries, and two separate rounds of research five months apart, not one seat is held by a rider advocate, a community organization representative, or a demographic-group delegate. This is the most robust finding in the analysis — it survived a full re-research and independent verification of every roster. In Day's US sample the same category reaches 50% (CTA Chicago) and 20% (MTA New York).

Medellín: Celebrated System, Political Board

Metro de Medellín is the most internationally celebrated system in the sample, and its board is 29% elected officials — the Mayor of Medellín and the Governor of Antioquia, both ex officio — with 14% transit operations expertise resting on a single judgment call. Two of its nine seats sit vacant. In the March snapshot the board had no transit expertise at all; the difference is one new Ministry of Transport appointee whose classification is genuinely arguable (see §6).

Buenos Aires: A Board of Generalists

SBASE — now SBASAU following its January 2026 conversion to a sociedad anónima — is the only board in the sample with no transit operations expertise whatsoever. All 6 directors classify as other management/policy. The March snapshot recorded this agency as having the highest transit expertise in the sample at 43%; that figure was wrong, and the correction is documented in §6.

Corporate Restructuring

Three of the five agencies operate as corporations with formal boards rather than as government departments: Metro S.A. (Chile) files with the securities regulator, Metrô de São Paulo publishes investor-relations disclosures, and SBASE was converted to a sociedad anónima in January 2026. This corporate form is what makes their board composition legible at all — CDMX, which is not structured this way, is correspondingly the hardest to document.

5Implications for NITA

Chicago's Northern Illinois Transit Authority (NITA) will replace the RTA in 2026, with new board appointments pending. Day's data shows that Chicago's current transit boards carry little transit operations expertise, leaning instead on generalist managers, elected officials, community advocates, and labor: CTA is 50% community advocates + 50% other management, RTA is 21% transit ops + 50% other management + 14% labor + 14% elected, and Metra is 9% transit + 55% other management + 9% community + 9% labor + 18% elected.

The LatAm data suggests five things for NITA:

  1. Generalist management is the global default, not a Chicago failing. At 71%, LatAm boards are more management-heavy than any region in Day's dataset. If NITA's goal is simply fewer politicians, that is achievable; if the goal is more transit expertise, appointing generalist professionals will not deliver it.
  1. Expertise requires an explicit mandate. No LatAm agency has a statutory requirement for transit-qualified directors, and none reliably has them. Where transit expertise appears it is incidental to the appointee rather than required by the seat.
  1. Ex officio seats import politics automatically. Medellín's two elected seats and Mexico City's cabinet-secretary seats exist because the statute ties board membership to another office. NITA should decide deliberately whether it wants that channel.
  1. Board composition is institutionally sticky. These five boards replaced most of their members in five months and produced nearly the same composition. Structure, not appointments, is the lever.
  1. Community voice doesn't have to mean zero expertise — but zero community voice is a real choice. LatAm sits at the opposite extreme from the US: no community representation anywhere. Neither extreme looks optimal. TfL London (13% transit / 56% other / 6% labor / 13% community / 13% elected) is the one board in Day's dataset that combines all four constituencies, and is the closest thing to a middle path on offer.

6Methodology & Limitations

How this was researched

Rosters were first assembled in March 2026 and fully re-researched on 27 August 2026. Every member record in data/ carries source URLs and a confidence rating. Primary sources were preferred throughout: securities filings (CMF Chile), investor-relations disclosures and integrated reports (Metrô de São Paulo), official gazettes (Boletín Oficial de la Ciudad de Buenos Aires, Gaceta Oficial de la Ciudad de México), and governor's decrees (Antioquia).

Corrections to the March 2026 version

The March analysis contained errors serious enough to change its headline findings. They are recorded here rather than quietly removed.

  1. Buenos Aires — a misattributed biography. Guillermo Seefeld was described as a former Secretary of Traffic and Transportation of Buenos Aires and former national Undersecretary of Land Transport, and classified as transit expertise on that basis. Those roles belong to Gustavo Álvarez, a different SBASE director. The source cited in support of the claim describes Seefeld only as a PRO party figure and the brother of an actor. This single error was responsible for the claim that Buenos Aires had the highest transit expertise in the sample (43%) and was "on par with Seoul Metro." It did not.
  1. São Paulo — three people who were not on the board. The March research could not reach Metrô's governance portal (HTTP 503) and fell back on a mirror site that was serving a 2023 roster. Cleyton Ricardo Batista, Luís Felipe Vidal Arellano and João Jorge Fadel Filho were listed as directors; none was on the board in March 2026. The official Integrated Report naming the correct members had been published four days before the research date.
  1. Mexico City — a misassigned seat. Rosario Castro Escorcia (Metrobús) was listed as holding the statutory seat reserved for the director of "Sistema de Movilidad 1." That is the former legal name of RTP, a different agency; the seat belongs to RTP's director.
  1. Day's own figures. Comparisons to Day's 16 agencies were originally read off his published chart image. They are now computed from the member-level list he published alongside his article. Three claims about his data were wrong: RTA Chicago's composition was misstated twice, Seoul Metro's 7% community-advocate share was described as elected officials, and the regional range table contained errors in the Asia, Europe and US rows.
  1. An unconfirmed member. Jorge Kogan was included in March as a "probable" board member inferred from a resolution signature. He is not on the current board.

Judgment calls

Seven classifications could reasonably have gone the other way. Each is recorded here and flagged in the member data, because the aggregate transit-expertise figure depends on them: classified as shown, transit operations expertise is 14%; under the stricter reading of all seven, it falls to 7%.

  • Rodrigo Troncoso Olchevskaia (Santiago) — classified Transit Ops/Management. Judgment call. An economist rather than an engineer, but his entire research output is transit economics — bus-industry cost and efficiency, fare policy, urban transport modelling. Treated as equivalent to the transit-academic carve-out in the classification rules.
  • Liliana Yanneth Bohórquez Avendaño (Medellín) — classified Transit Ops/Management. Judgment call. A civil engineer with ~25 years across Bogotá's mobility secretariat, the national road-safety agency and the Ministry of Transport, who also sits on the board of Metrolínea, Bucaramanga's BRT operator. The case against counting her is that traffic engineering and road safety are not transit operations; the case for is a quarter-century inside transport bodies plus a seat at a transit operator. Counted as transit expertise.
  • Adrián Rubalcava Suárez (Mexico City) — classified Elected Official. Judgment call. He sits as an appointed director general, not as an officeholder, which would argue for Other Management/Policy. He is counted as an elected official because he was mayor of Cuajimalpa until 2024 and is a career politician, and because this matches how Day treated the same situation — his own data counts former mayors and appointed cabinet officials sitting on US and European boards as elected officials.
  • César Cravioto Romero (Mexico City) — classified Elected Official. Judgment call. A former Morena senator and party faction coordinator now sitting as an appointed cabinet secretary. Counted as an elected official on the same basis as Rubalcava, and consistent with Day's treatment of Berlin's appointed transport senator on the BVG board.
  • Daniel Rodrigues Aldigueri (São Paulo) — classified Transit Ops/Management. Judgment call. Holds a doctorate in Transportation and a master's in Transportation Engineering, but has spent his career in ports and civil aviation rather than urban transit. Structural limitations

  • The comparison is not date-matched. The five LatAm boards are current as of 27 August 2026. Day's 16 agencies are as of March 2026 and have not been re-verified here; some have likely changed.
  • Board composition is volatile. Four of these five boards changed membership within five months. Any snapshot goes stale quickly, and agency websites lag reality — Metro de Medellín's own board page listed a member weeks after the decree replacing him.
  • Three CDMX seats are institutional, not individual. The federal transport ministry, Banobras and State of Mexico representatives could not be identified by name and are counted as institutional seats.
  • Not every claim is equally sourced. Records carry High, Medium or Low confidence. Low-confidence records should be treated as leads rather than findings.
  • Category boundaries are contestable. Day's five categories were designed for his sample; applying them to state-owned enterprises in a different legal tradition requires judgment at the margins, as §6's seven calls illustrate.
